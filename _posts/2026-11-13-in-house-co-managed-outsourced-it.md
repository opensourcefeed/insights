## What Is In-House, Co-Managed, and Outsourced IT

Depending on their size, business requirements, and internal resources, modern enterprises structure their IT support in different ways. For example, the most commonly used models are in-house IT, outsourced IT, and co-managed IT, and the key difference between them lies in who actually performs the IT tasks and who is responsible for the results.

In-house IT means that all key IT functions are performed by the company's in-house staff, whereas outsourced IT involves transferring all IT functions to an external provider, most often an MSP. Co-managed IT, in turn, combines both approaches: some tasks remain the responsibility of the internal team, while an external partner supplements the existing resources. Below, we present a comparison table that will help you clearly understand the difference between these three models.

| **Model** | **Who Performs the Work** | **Where Responsibility Lies** | **Typical Use Cases** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **In-House IT** | In-house IT team | Within the company | Companies that need ongoing internal oversight and their own IT expertise |
| **Outsourced IT** | External MSP | Primarily provided by an external provider in accordance with the agreed scope of services | Companies without their own IT team or those who want to outsource IT management to an external partner |
| **Co-Managed IT** | In-house team and external provider | Shared between the company and the MSP | Companies that have in-house IT professionals but need additional resources, expertise, or support |

It's important to understand that none of these models is a one-size-fits-all solution for every business, and the choice between them depends on the company's size, available IT resources, the level of control required, and how quickly the business plans to scale.

## Signs Your Business Has Outgrown Its Current IT Model

It often happens that a company uses the same IT model for years without understanding the reasons behind delays, security issues, and a lack of time for long-term planning. Most often, the problem lies in the model itself; even if it was ideal in the early stages of the company's development, as the number of employees, locations, system, and business processes grows, [managed IT support](https://www.itgoat.com/it-service-models/it-outsourcing-services/) may become necessary. The signs below indicate that now is the time to reevaluate your current approach to IT.

### Ticket backlogs and slow response times

This is one of the most obvious signs that the current IT business model is outdated and hindering the company's growth. As a company grows, in-house specialists or basic contractors can no longer keep up with the flow of routine and complex tasks. In such cases, even simple requests, such as resetting a password or configuring access, take days instead of minutes to resolve, and critical outages are fixed too slowly. Thus, while an employee waits for an IT issue to be resolved, the company pays their salary but receives no results. Moreover, without receiving timely assistance, departments begin to purchase software on their own, set up cloud storage, and use personal accounts, all of which create security vulnerabilities. What is more, if long response times affect customer service, the business directly loses money and customer loyalty.

### Rising cybersecurity and compliance demands

A business has outgrown its current IT model when security and compliance shift from being background tasks to becoming the primary obstacles to growth. At this stage, basic antivirus software and fragmented policies are no longer sufficient to protect data and pass audits. Insurance companies deny coverage or change exorbitant premiums because the company's infrastructure lacks basic standards such as multi-factor authentication or disk encryption. Employees independently sign up for neural networks, cloud storage services, and messaging apps, uploading trade secrets and customer databases to them. The current IT model fails to detect these leaks and cannot prevent them. Furthermore, backups are created, but no one has verified how quickly the business can be restored from them following a serious attack. Downtime in the event of a failure can lead to critical losses.

### Lack of strategic IT leadership

This means that the IT department functions solely as a technical support service rather than as a tool for business development, and as the company scales, this reactive approach inevitably hinders growth, increases costs, and creates critical risks. In such cases, IT specialists are occupied exclusively with fixing current glitches and configuring workstations, leaving the team with absolutely no time for planning, automation, and implementing improvements. Management views technology solely as an expense for hardware and licenses, and the company lacks an understanding of how IT tools can directly increase revenue, accelerate sales, or reduce production costs. Furthermore, management doesn't understand exactly where the money is going and cannot forecast technology expenses when expanding the workforce or launching new products, often resulting in the purchase of unnecessary licenses or excess server capacity.
