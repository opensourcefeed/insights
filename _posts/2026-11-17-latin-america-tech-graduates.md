Brazil's universities graduated 110,960 people in computing and ICT in 2024, according to the Brasscom labor market report from May 2026. That is 59.1% more than in 2022, when the number was about 70,000. No other country in the region publishes figures at this scale, so Brazil is the best place to see how the pipeline works.

The numbers raise a better question than "how many?". Where do these engineers learn, and what happens after they graduate?

![Students working in a university computer lab](/insights/assets/images/post-images/latin-america-tech-graduates.webp)


## Universities do most of the work

Across Ibero-America, which includes Spain and Portugal, higher education reached 34.1 million students in 2022. That is 30% more than a decade earlier. Nearly 5 million people graduated that year, but only 20% of them studied STEM subjects, according to the Organization of Ibero-American States (OEI).

Brazil does better than that average in tech. About 40% of its 2024 ICT graduates studied systems analysis and development, a practical degree aimed at building applications. The federal technical schools add another 12,930 graduates, which brings the total to about 124,000.

Brasscom says Brazil is not short of graduates or vacancies. The weak point is the step between the diploma and a formal job. Most of the 111,100 tech workers added between 2023 and 2025 found work as freelancers or in informal roles, not on a salaried contract.

## Bootcamps and public programs fill the gaps

Universities do not reach everyone, so governments and private schools run faster programs.

- **Colombia:** In March 2025 the technology ministry opened 3,985 more places in its free Talento TECH bootcamps in Boyacá and Cundinamarca. Nearly 6,000 people there had already been certified.
- **Dominican Republic:** The telecom regulator INDOTEL launched 30,000 digital skills scholarships in August 2025.
- **El Salvador:** The Inter-American Development Bank and Google Cloud announced a pilot of 10,000 digital training scholarships in December 2025.

Bootcamps work well for people who already know what they want to learn. They teach people to write code every day. They teach less about reading and maintaining someone else's code, which is most of a professional developer's job.

## What this means if you are hiring

A graduate certificate shows that someone learned the basics. It does not show that they have shipped software. That gap is why many companies work with a partner for <a href="https://www.n-ix.com/team-extension-in-latin-america/" rel="sponsored">IT staff augmentation Latin America</a> instead of hiring juniors one by one. A good partner pairs new graduates with senior engineers who have worked in production.

Seniors do not come out of any graduating class. Every one of the 110,000 Brazilian graduates starts near the bottom of the ladder, so a team made only of them has no one to learn from.

Before you sign with any vendor, ask for:

- The schools and programs their engineers came from, by name.
- Examples of shipped work, which count for more than certificates.
- A plan for mentoring juniors in their first months.
- An English test in a live conversation, not a line on a resume.

## The short version

The supply of tech graduates in Latin America is large and growing, and Brazil alone cleared 110,000 in 2024. Universities carry most of the load. Bootcamps and public programs reach people the universities do not. The harder part is turning graduates into experienced engineers, so ask where a team's engineers trained before you ask what they cost.
