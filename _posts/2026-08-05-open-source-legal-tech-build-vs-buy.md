**For** years, legal technology followed a familiar pattern: law firms purchased expensive proprietary software, adapted their workflows to fit the platform, and waited for the vendor's next feature release.

That model is beginning to change.

A growing number of lawyers, developers, and legal technology companies are embracing open source software, creating a new wave of customizable legal tools that firms can inspect, modify, and build upon themselves.

![Open source legal technology tools and law firm software development](/insights/assets/images/post-images/open-source-legal-tech.webp)

## AI Has Changed the Economics of Legal Software

Historically, building legal software required significant engineering resources.

Today, AI-assisted coding has dramatically lowered that barrier.

Lawyers with a clear understanding of legal workflows can increasingly collaborate with developers—or even prototype applications themselves—without building everything from scratch.

As a result, the competitive advantage is shifting away from generic software and toward proprietary legal expertise, workflows, and knowledge.

## Firms Want Greater Control

Many firms are becoming more selective about where their legal data resides.

Open source platforms allow organizations to:

- self-host sensitive information;
- inspect source code;
- customize workflows;
- integrate internal knowledge bases;
- avoid vendor lock-in; and
- build practice-area-specific tools.

For firms with unique regulatory or industry practices, this flexibility can be particularly valuable.

## The Value Is Moving Up the Stack

Interestingly, the software itself may become less valuable than what firms build on top of it.

Two firms using the same open source platform may create entirely different systems based on their:

- drafting standards;
- negotiation playbooks;
- regulatory knowledge;
- document templates;
- internal precedents;
- review processes; and
- client workflows.

In other words, the competitive advantage increasingly lies in legal judgment rather than the underlying code.

## Open Source Doesn't Mean "No Intellectual Property"

One common misconception is that open source software has no intellectual property implications.

In reality, [open source projects](/software/) are distributed under licences that specify how the software may be:

- used;
- modified;
- distributed;
- sublicensed; and
- incorporated into commercial products.

Businesses adopting or contributing to open source projects should understand the applicable licence terms before integrating them into client-facing products or proprietary platforms.

## Collaboration Is Accelerating Innovation

Another interesting development is the willingness of legal professionals to collaborate.

Open source legal AI projects now include document review systems, contract analysis tools, legal knowledge platforms, and workflow automation frameworks that are continuously improved by contributors around the world.

Rather than every firm independently solving the same infrastructure problems, many are beginning to share foundational technology while differentiating themselves through expertise and client service.

## The Future May Be Hybrid

Proprietary legal technology is unlikely to disappear.

Instead, many firms may adopt a hybrid model by combining commercial AI products with internally developed open source tools tailored to their own practice.

That approach allows firms to benefit from commercial innovation while retaining greater control over sensitive workflows and intellectual property.

## Don't Forget the Legal Side of Open Source

As organizations increasingly build and deploy open source legal technology, they should also consider issues such as:

- open source licence compliance;
- copyright ownership;
- contributor agreements;
- software licensing;
- AI-generated code;
- confidentiality; and
- commercial distribution.

Understanding who owns the code—and under what licence it can be used—is just as important as writing the code itself.

Businesses and legal technology companies developing or commercializing open source software should understand how copyright and software licensing interact.
